The Atrophy of Judgment

Megi Pishtari

In a week-long experiment published in 2026, Anthropic built a marketplace where Claude agents negotiated real purchases and sales on behalf of their owners. The agents closed 186 deals worth just over four thousand dollars. In a parallel version, some people were represented by a stronger model and some by a weaker one, without being told which.

The people with weaker agents got measurably worse deals. They did not notice. Asked afterward how fair their transactions had been, the two groups answered almost identically. On Anthropic’s 1-to-7 scale, where 4 means fair to both parties and the poles mean unfair to one side or the other, both groups sat at the midpoint: 4.05 and 4.06. The disadvantaged could not feel the disadvantage. A person can lose ground through an agent and still lack the evaluative position from which to recognize the loss.

That is where the deeper problem begins. AI may be changing the faculty by which human beings are meant to govern its use: judgment.

Judgment is the slower and less visible labor that precedes choice: the work of noticing what matters, framing the question correctly, sensing where a summary is too smooth, a recommendation too confident, a conclusion arrived at before thought has fully done its work. Judgment is what lets a person say not only this seems useful, but something about this is wrong, even when the error has not yet declared itself.

That faculty is easiest to appreciate when it fails. A colleague forwards an AI-generated executive summary to a room full of decision-makers without opening the document it claims to represent. The summary is clean, coherent, plausible. What it omits is a qualification buried deep in the original text, the one complication that should have changed the decision. Nobody notices. The summary is good enough. A friend admits over dinner that she no longer writes most of her own emails. A language model drafts; she approves. I ask whether the messages still sound like her. She pauses, then says she cannot always tell. She seems relieved, not alarmed. In both cases, something distinctly human has been surrendered before anyone experiences the surrender as a loss.

The problem is structural. Many AI systems, especially large language models and autonomous agents, intervene at a level earlier tools never reached. They go further than helping us execute decisions. They offer articulation before thought, framing before judgment, and closure before attention has fully had a chance to work.

People will keep choosing. What deteriorates is the condition under which choosing remains meaningful. Repeated assistance may shift experienced users from active judgment toward monitoring and approving the system’s output, a form of cognitive offloading that can be difficult to notice from inside the task. What disappears is not action but its silent partner: a certain quality of attention.

We do not yet have a good public language for this condition. Existing terms capture parts of it. Automation bias names our tendency to over-trust machine outputs. Deskilling names the loss of competence when a task is simplified or outsourced. Learned helplessness names what repeated failure can do to initiative. But none of these quite describes the slow deformation now underway in white-collar, professional, and institutional life: the incremental surrender of evaluative authority through a sequence of individually reasonable acts. One accepts the summary instead of reading the source. One lets the system suggest the frame of the problem before forming a view of one’s own. Nothing dramatic happens. The day becomes smoother. The work gets done. And posture, sustained long enough, becomes identity.

I will call this agential drift: the gradual weakening of a person’s capacity to exercise judgment through habitual reliance on systems that pre-structure thought. What is being lost is something more fragile and more practical than intelligence or abstract freedom: the discipline of forming one’s own view before accepting a ready-made one. Reading becomes receiving. Authoring becomes approving.

The question, then, is what happens when a civilization begins to prefer assisted articulation to the strenuous formation of judgment, and when institutions built on the presumption of human oversight start to erode the very powers that oversight requires. That question is moral and political before it is technical.

· · ·

Return for a moment to the people in that marketplace who could not feel their disadvantage. They were not coerced and they were not asleep. They kept every formal power to judge, and still could not perceive what had been done in their name. To understand how that is possible, and why it is not the same as ordinary error, it helps to be exact about what freedom under constraint actually demands.

Beauvoir is useful here because she refuses two errors at once. She refuses the fantasy that human beings float free of circumstance, choosing from nowhere. And she refuses the opposite fantasy, that because conditions shape us, we are therefore mere objects to whom events happen. Freedom, for Beauvoir, is situated. It lives under conditions not of our own making: economic pressure, bodily vulnerability, institutional limits, political force. But it still demands exercise. The moral life begins in the difficult space where one is neither omnipotent nor excused.

This matters because discussions of AI often flatten very different kinds of human exposure into one story. A worker subject to AI-monitored production quotas does not stand in the same relation to freedom as a professional who uncritically accepts a generated summary instead of reading the underlying source. The first may be constrained by a system she did not choose, cannot redesign, and risks punishment by resisting. The second may still possess room for judgment, yet decline to use it. Beauvoir helps restore the distinction. Some forms of technological dependence foreclose possibility from the outside. Others tempt people to surrender powers they still have.

Most of us inhabit the middle ground. We work inside institutions that have already integrated AI into ordinary workflow, and within those limits we continue to make a hundred small daily decisions; whether to read the source or the summary, whether to let the model’s phrasing stand, whether to notice that a recommendation is too eager to collapse a problem before it has been properly opened. No one choice is decisive. The deformation enters as a habit.

Murdoch sharpens the point. For her, freedom appears earlier than the moment of choice; in the quality of attention one brings to reality. A system can preserve the formal appearance of human decision while thinning the consciousness from which good decisions arise. The user retains veto power. The deeper practice of noticing what deserves resistance erodes underneath.

Some systems narrow the field by presenting institutional judgments as discoveries. But large language models pose a more intimate temptation. They do not merely rank or sort. They speak. They offer wording in one’s own approximate voice. They relieve the user of the burden of articulation while preserving the appearance of authorship. The danger goes deeper than simple error. A person encounters a sentence under her own name that she did not wrestle into being, and gradually loses sensitivity to the difference.

Autonomous agents extend this one step further. They do not just suggest; they proceed. That makes a looser supervisory posture possible. Instead of evaluating each action as it occurs, the user can wait for an obvious failure before intervening. The risk is that exception-handling gradually substitutes for active judgment. That risk is an inference rather than a finding: the work on skill decay under assistance concerns performing a task with help, not supervising a system that performs it, and nobody has studied the second directly. One might call this a movement from anticipatory judgment to approval, and from approval to interruption. The question is no longer “What is happening here, and how should it be framed?” but “Do I see anything bad enough to stop?”

What these systems share is a capacity to pre-structure thought. They offer language before speech, criteria before judgment, salience before attention has sorted the field for itself. They do not abolish freedom. They make freedom less likely to be exercised well. And because the resulting outputs are often competent, fluent, and socially rewarded, the user experiences the shift as relief.

· · ·

If this sounds like a merely philosophical anxiety, the early empirical record suggests otherwise.

The clearest findings show something disorienting: people often perform better with assistance while quietly losing the ability to perform without it. A randomized controlled trial in a single Turkish high school, reported by Bastani and colleagues as Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics (PNAS, 2025), gave nearly a thousand mathematics students one of three conditions: an interface built to mimic ChatGPT, a tutor version prompted to withhold the answer and offer hints instead, or no access at all. Both assisted groups did far better on the practice problems, by 48% and 127% respectively. On an exam afterward, taken without any assistance, the students given the ChatGPT-like interface scored 17% below the control, and the students given the safeguarded tutor were statistically indistinguishable from it. The harm sits in the unrestricted condition rather than in assistance as such. Neither assisted group could see its own result: the students who had done worse did not perceive that they had performed worse or learned less, and the students who had done no better than the control believed they had done significantly better. The trial ran in late 2023, on GPT-4, in one school, which is a narrower base than the argument it is usually asked to carry.

A similar pattern appears among experts, though the evidence there is of a weaker kind. Budzyń and colleagues, writing in The Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology in 2025, reported a retrospective before-and-after analysis at four Polish centers: it compared non-AI colonoscopies performed before routine AI adoption with AI-off colonoscopies performed after the endoscopists had begun using AI regularly. Nineteen endoscopists, each with more than two thousand prior colonoscopies and a mean of twenty-seven years behind them, across 1,443 unassisted procedures. The adenoma detection rate, meaning the proportion of colonoscopies that find at least one adenoma, fell from 28.4% to 22.4%: six percentage points on a 95% confidence interval from 10.5 to 1.6, or about a fifth of the rate it started from, which is one result stated twice rather than two results. Cancer detection did not significantly change. This was not evidence that AI simply made colonoscopy worse: trials have found that it improves adenoma detection while active. Budzyń’s narrower finding was that, once AI became routine, the same endoscopists detected fewer adenomas when working without it. The design is observational, so it cannot separate the effect of the tool from whatever else changed across those six months. And whether the endoscopists noticed is not a question the study asked: they knew when the system was on, and nothing recorded whether they saw their own detection rate fall.

The study of software developers is a trial, and the asymmetry in it is sharper. In Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity (METR, July 2025), 246 tasks were randomized across sixteen experienced developers, each working on an open-source repository they had maintained for an average of five years, with the AI tools of early 2025 permitted on some tasks and forbidden on others. Before starting, the developers forecast that the tools would cut their completion time by 24%. Afterward, they estimated the tools had cut it by 20%. The measured effect was that the tasks took 19% longer. Those are two beliefs and a measurement, and they must not be combined: adding a slowdown to a perceived speedup produces a figure no study reports.

Two things limit how far that carries. The 19% is a point estimate on a 95% confidence interval that METR’s own February 2026 restatement gives as +2% to +39%, so what the trial establishes is that these developers were slowed, not by how much. And it measures one season: the tools available between February and June 2025, in mature repositories, with sixteen people. METR ran a second experiment from August 2025, on later tools and a larger and only partly overlapping group, and reported in February 2026 that its raw estimates came out with the opposite sign — an 18% speedup among the returning developers, 4% among the newly recruited, both on intervals that cross zero. It published those figures alongside its own judgment that the experiment was too compromised to support a confident estimate: developers increasingly declined to work without AI, those who took part held back the tasks they least wanted to attempt unassisted, the pay was lower, and time was hard to measure for anyone running several agents at once. METR calls its own new numbers very weak evidence.

That is not a retraction of the first result, and this essay should not treat it as one. The 19% stands for those sixteen developers, those tasks and those tools. What does not stand is carrying it forward as a current or portable estimate of what AI does to software work, which is what citing it in 2026 without any of this would have done. What lasts is the calibration failure. The developers were confident about a productivity effect they could not estimate, before the tasks and again afterward, and being wrong in the other direction later would not repair that.

A system may increase output while degrading calibration. Confidence without calibration is not competence. And when the tool designed to be overseen begins weakening the faculties of the people meant to oversee it, the problem becomes recursive; and institutional.

· · ·

The institutional stakes are higher still. In private life, the cost of weak judgment may be a bad email, a thinner understanding of a text, a habit of reaching for generated language too quickly. In institutions, the same drift can shape decisions about funding, hiring, sentencing, treatment, eligibility, and oversight. The most dangerous effects of AI may appear where the technology becomes ordinary inside systems that already reduce messy realities to legible categories. Institutions have always translated lives into forms, cases, metrics, and thresholds. AI enters this environment as an accelerant.

The deeper institutional danger is degraded oversight. In high-stakes work, judgment at its best operates in anticipatory mode: one looks for where trouble might be developing before it has declared itself. Approval mode is already weaker: the human signs off unless something appears obviously amiss. Interrupt mode is weaker still: the system runs, and the human intervenes only when a problem becomes undeniable. The movement from anticipation to approval to interruption can happen without coercion. It requires only comfort, only the repeated experience that the tool is usually good enough and that deeper scrutiny is cognitively expensive.

I have seen versions of this problem in institutional life long before AI became fashionable enough to name it clearly. The moments that mattered most were rarely the ones with clean dashboards. They were the conversations that complicated them. In one internal review, the data indicated successful beneficiary reach in a politically sensitive region. On paper, the operation looked compliant. The numbers were correct. Yet the language in the local reporting felt strained, too polished, too uniform. A senior colleague paused the conversation and said: I want to hear this described by someone who was there. The call that followed changed the picture. Access had been negotiated under fragile conditions. Local leaders had acquiesced publicly while resisting privately. The figures remained accurate. The situation was not stable. No dashboard would have surfaced that difference. It required a specifically human disposition: suspicion of excessive coherence, and the willingness to slow a process down when everything appears to be running smoothly.

That disposition is exactly what AI systems are poorly designed to preserve. Their comparative advantage is fluent closure. They offer not only outputs but frames: this is the salient variable, this is the likely interpretation, this is the paragraph, this is the shortlist. And once the frame is supplied, much of the real intellectual work has already been done on the user’s behalf. The most consequential delegation may therefore occur before any decision is made. Sometimes what is surrendered is not the verdict but the authority to decide what counts as relevant before the verdict is reached.

In organizational settings, this loss is compounded by diffusion of responsibility. When an AI-shaped judgment proves thin or mistaken, responsibility spreads across the chain: the analyst points to the model, the manager to the dashboard, the vendor to the data, the institution to the need for efficiency. No one need lie. The failure can remain entirely procedural.

The loss also reaches epistemic humility. Good institutions depend on people willing to say that the data may be right while the situation is wrong, that a recommendation may be coherent and still not deserve assent. But a system optimized for convenience will always treat this sort of friction as a defect. Over time, the institution begins to confuse the smoothness of a process with the soundness of a judgment. And once that confusion sets in, the human role remains in place while the human faculty that gave the role meaning begins to thin.

· · ·

The language of alignment is meant to reassure. It suggests that the central problem is making AI systems conform to human values, intentions, and preferences. There is something sensible in this ambition. But taken alone, it is too shallow for the problem now before us.

The shallowness lies in what the frame assumes. It assumes that human preferences are stable enough to serve as inputs, that what a user wants can be read off from behavior and satisfaction. But preferences are not static deposits waiting to be extracted. They are shaped by repetition, by convenience, by available defaults, by the texture of the tools through which one moves. A person who has spent months relying on frictionless drafting assistance may come sincerely to prefer cleaner phrasing, smoother transitions, and faster closure. But if the system helped produce those preferences, then aligning to them is not necessarily alignment with an independent human will. It may be a kind of loop: the machine training the taste to which it is then praised for conforming.

The more important question is whether AI preserves the conditions under which values can still be formed, revised, and resisted. What alignment ought to protect is the conditions under which authentic preference formation remains possible: the openness of situations, the exercise of reflective endorsement, the sustained practice of attention, the capacity for genuine self-determination. Alignment, properly understood, means preserving the space in which a person can still become answerable for what she desires in the first place.

Seen in this light, user satisfaction is a dangerously weak proxy. The systems most corrosive of judgment may well be those that feel best to use. What preserves agency often feels worse in the moment than what erodes it. A civilization that makes convenience its highest design value should not be surprised when it begins to lose the faculties that inconvenience once exercised.

A human being is more than a bundle of preferences to be mirrored back efficiently. Judgment is a formed capacity. It lives in habits of attention, hesitation, self-correction, and resistance to premature closure. It depends on pre-decisional space: the interval in which the mind has not yet accepted the frame of the question, the wording of the answer, or the salience of the evidence. Evaluation is downstream of articulation. Once a system has proposed the paragraph, framed the issue, or supplied the recommendation, much of the formative labor has already been displaced. Systems should be judged by whether sustained use strengthens or weakens the user’s own powers of judgment. Few governance frameworks measure this directly.

· · ·

The choice is not between using AI and refusing it. The real question is whether we will build and inhabit tools in ways that preserve judgment, or whether we will come to regard judgment itself as an inefficient residue of a slower age. The most seductive story now on offer is that the burdens of articulation, evaluation, and deliberation can be progressively lifted without anything essential being lost. The burden is real. The relief is real. What is less often admitted is that the burden was doing formative work.

Education, at its best, was never merely the delivery of information. It was the cultivation of a person capable of staying with difficulty long enough for thought to become her own. Writing existed to discover, through the labor of articulation, what one actually thinks. Judgment existed to form the habits by which one notices when the options themselves are badly framed.

This is why some kinds of friction are protections. To require a person to draft before consulting the model, to record a judgment before viewing the recommendation, to read the source before accepting the summary: these are practices by which judgment stays alive. The helpful friction feels wasteful only if one has already decided that the point of thinking is to arrive at outputs with maximum speed. But if the point is to remain the sort of being who can still answer for those outputs, then the friction is exercise.

The deeper task is existential before it is technical. We do not need only better AI literacy or better disclosure regimes. We need the formation of dispositions that refuse premature closure: the willingness to read past the summary, to distrust excessive coherence, to notice when convenience is quietly reordering conscience. If we build systems and organizations that reward approval mode and frictionless assent, we will train people accordingly and call the result adaptation. If we want judgment to survive, we will have to design for it, protect it, and sometimes inconvenience ourselves on its behalf.

The temptation of the present moment is subtler than forced passivity. Machines will offer us an increasingly elegant exit from the strain of thinking, and we will mistake the relief for progress. The day gets smoother. The recommendation easier to defend. A culture that steadily prefers assisted articulation to formed judgment may eventually retain all the outward signs of intelligence while losing the inward habits that made intelligence answerable to reality.

That is the atrophy this essay has tried to name. Thought does not disappear. It thins into approval. Freedom is not abolished. It is exercised less and less well, until the exercise begins to seem unnecessary.

The question is whether we still want to remain the kinds of creatures for whom judgment is worth preserving, even when preservation feels slower, less satisfying, and harder to measure than convenience. If we do, then the uncertain, effortful, sometimes humiliating labor of thinking for ourselves is not a residue to be engineered away. It is the price of remaining answerable for what we say, what we choose, and what we become.
