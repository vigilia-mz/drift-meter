The Drift Meter

Evaluating the evaluator under AI assistance

The Drift Meter is an instrument for evaluating the evaluator. It does not ask only whether AI improves an answer. It asks what AI assistance changes in the person responsible for judging the answer: their attention, confidence, assumptions, tolerance for ambiguity, and willingness to revise.

The underlying concept is agential drift: the incremental transfer of evaluative authority to AI through delegations that each look reasonable. What it costs is the judgment that oversight depends on.

What it measures

Evidence engagement, revision behavior, ambiguity tolerance, framing autonomy and confidence calibration — traces of how the same review is done in two conditions, once with an estimate supplied and once with none.

Two further measures ask whether the answer was right, so that a run can count against this project’s own hypothesis rather than only confirm it. Neither can carry a result yet. Estimate accuracy stays undefined until a supported value is written for each assumption, and inventing those values to complete it would be the failure this project is about. The planted-error catch rate rests on one planted error per slate, which for a single reader is one observation rather than a rate. The protocol keeps both, with their reasons.

The supplied estimate is credited, at random, to an AI assistant, to a senior programme officer, or to an unnamed prior reviewer. The number, the reasoning and the recommendation are identical in all three. Only the authority attached to them changes, which is what separates a claim about AI from a claim about handed answers in general. Why it matters

As AI systems become more capable, the human role often moves upstream: from execution to framing, monitoring, approval, and responsibility. That makes human judgment more important, not less. But it also makes judgment harder to evaluate.

A person may receive a better output while becoming less able to explain, challenge, or independently reconstruct the reasoning behind it. They may feel more confident while their evaluative range narrows. They may approve work without seeing what has changed.

The Drift Meter begins from that tension. It treats oversight as a human capacity, not only a governance process.

The larger project

At the institutional level, the question becomes one of evaluator integrity — structural, not moral: the intact capacity to frame, question, reject, revise, and take responsibility for AI-assisted work. The goal is not to discourage AI use. It is to help people and institutions adopt AI without quietly outsourcing the capacities they still need most.

A note on method

This is a prototype, and it is built to claim only what it can observe. It does not score whether your decisions were right. It has not been run at scale, so it reports no cohort findings — an earlier version showed an invented cohort dashboard, and I removed it, because a piece about the costs of unearned confidence should not manufacture its own. What remains is a trace of one person’s judgment across one sitting, and the questions that trace raises.
