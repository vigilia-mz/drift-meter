The Drift Meter

Evaluating the evaluator under AI assistance

The Drift Meter is an experiment in evaluating the evaluator. It does not ask only whether AI improves an answer. It asks what AI assistance changes in the person responsible for judging the answer: their attention, confidence, assumptions, tolerance for ambiguity, and willingness to revise.

The underlying concept is agential drift: the gradual weakening of evaluative independence through small, reasonable acts of delegation.

What it measures

Framing autonomy, ambiguity tolerance, evidence engagement, revision behavior, and confidence calibration — the same review completed in two conditions, once with AI assistance and once without it.

Why it matters

As AI systems become more capable, the human role often moves upstream: from execution to framing, monitoring, approval, and responsibility. That makes human judgment more important, not less. But it also makes judgment harder to evaluate.

A person may receive a better output while becoming less able to explain, challenge, or independently reconstruct the reasoning behind it. They may feel more confident while their evaluative range narrows. They may approve work without seeing what has changed.

The Drift Meter begins from that tension. It treats oversight as a human capacity, not only a governance process.

The larger project

At the institutional level, the question becomes one of evaluator integrity — structural, not moral: the intact capacity to frame, question, reject, revise, and take responsibility for AI-assisted work. The goal is not to discourage AI use. It is to help people and institutions adopt AI without quietly outsourcing the capacities they still need most.

A note on method

This is a prototype, and it is built to claim only what it can observe. It does not score whether your decisions were right. It has not been run at scale, so it reports no cohort findings — an earlier version showed an invented cohort dashboard, and I removed it, because a piece about the costs of unearned confidence should not manufacture its own. What remains is a trace of one person’s judgment across one short task, and the questions that trace raises.
